# Lesson 1.4.1 — Major muscles and how they are named

**Term 1, Module 1, Chapter 1.4, Lesson 1.** Completes Module 1, and directly feeds the Lat Pulldown Lab's prime-mover/contributor/stabiliser toggles (Chapter 4.6).

## Chapter framing

**Why it matters:** the Lat Pulldown Lab — Term 1's flagship interactive piece — asks a learner to toggle between primary movers, contributors and stabilisers on a real exercise. None of that means anything without this lesson first: naming the major muscles involved, and understanding what "prime mover" versus a supporting role actually means.

**Learning objectives:**
1. Name major exercise-relevant muscles at an introductory level.
2. Explain at least two muscle-naming conventions (this lesson teaches three: location, size, and number of origins), with an example of each.
3. Distinguish prime mover, synergist and stabilising synergist (fixator), and map these to the friendlier labels the Lat Pulldown Lab uses.

**Learner capabilities (what a learner can do afterwards):**
- Name a handful of major muscles a learner is likely to hear referenced in exercise contexts.
- Explain why a muscle like "biceps brachii" or "gluteus maximus" is named the way it is.
- Given a described role in a movement, say whether it sounds like a prime mover, a contributor, or a stabiliser.

**Prerequisites:** [1.1.1](term-01-module-1-chapter-1-lesson-1.md), [1.2.1](term-01-module-1-chapter-2-lesson-1.md), [1.3.1](term-01-module-1-chapter-3-lesson-1.md).

**Keywords:** prime mover, synergist, stabiliser, origin, insertion, muscle-naming conventions.

**Concept map (textual; a visual diagram is a later design task, not built here):**
```
Muscle names come from conventions
   ├─ Location (e.g. frontalis — over the frontal bone)
   ├─ Size (e.g. gluteus maximus/medius/minimus — largest to smallest)
   └─ Number of origins (e.g. bi- = two, tri- = three: biceps, triceps)

Muscles play different roles in a movement
   ├─ Prime mover (OpenStax: "agonist") — the muscle mainly responsible
   ├─ Synergist — assists the prime mover
   │     └─ Fixator — a synergist that stabilises a bone so the prime mover has a steady base
   └─ Antagonist — has the opposite action to the prime mover
          ↓
   Fit360's Lat Pulldown Lab labels (Chapter 4.6):
   primary mover = prime mover · contributor = synergist · stabiliser = fixator
```

**Safety and scope boundary:** this chapter names and classifies muscles and their roles in a movement generally; it never assesses an individual's muscle development, strength, imbalance or injury. Observing "my biceps contracts here" is naming, not a fitness or health assessment. The standard Module 0 boundary applies throughout (see [Lesson 0.1.1](term-01-module-0-chapter-1-lesson-1.md) and [0.3.1](term-01-module-0-chapter-3-lesson-1.md)).

**Diagnostic quiz (ungraded, before teaching, to surface assumptions):**
1. True or False: "biceps" and "triceps" are named for their location, not their structure. *(False — the bi-/tri- prefix names the number of origins: two and three.)*
2. Multiple choice: in a movement, the muscle mainly responsible for it is called the — (a) synergist, (b) prime mover, (c) antagonist. *(b.)*
3. True or False: a stabiliser and a prime mover play the same role in a movement. *(False — a stabiliser supports the movement without being the main driver of it.)*

## Lesson body

**Question:** When I hear a muscle's name, or hear it described as "doing the work" versus "helping out," what does that actually mean?

**Explanation:**
- **Naming conventions** (OpenStax, *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*, §11.2, "Naming Skeletal Muscles"): anatomists name muscles using several criteria, including location, shape, size, number of origins, and action. Three with clear, memorable examples:
  - **Location** — a muscle can be named for where it sits, or what it attaches to. OpenStax's example: the *frontalis* sits over the frontal bone.
  - **Size** — the gluteal muscles are named by relative size: *gluteus maximus* (largest), *gluteus medius* (medium), *gluteus minimus* (smallest).
  - **Number of origins** — the prefix *bi-* means two origins, *tri-* means three: *biceps brachii* (two-headed muscle of the upper arm) and *triceps brachii* (three-headed).
- **Major exercise-relevant muscles**, named at an introductory level: latissimus dorsi, deltoids, pectoralis major, biceps brachii, triceps brachii, trapezius, quadriceps, hamstrings, and the gluteal muscles. These are standard, widely used anatomical names, not specific to any exercise brand or method.
- **Roles muscles play in a movement** (OpenStax, §11.1, "Interactions of Skeletal Muscles..."): the muscle mainly responsible for a movement is the **prime mover**, or **agonist**. Muscles that assist are **synergists**. A synergist that stabilises a bone — giving the prime mover a steady base to work from — is specifically called a **fixator**. A muscle with the opposite action to the prime mover is the **antagonist**.
- **Bridging to the Lat Pulldown Lab's vocabulary:** the [Lat Pulldown Lab](../labs/lat-pulldown-lab.md) (Chapter 4.6) uses slightly more accessible labels for these same roles, not OpenStax's exact terms: **primary mover** (= prime mover), **contributor** (= synergist), and **stabiliser** (= fixator). This lesson teaches both sets of terms side by side, so a learner recognises the Lab's language as soon as they reach it, rather than meeting an unexplained new vocabulary there.

**Visual/lab:** no interactive lab is attached to Chapter 1.4 itself (the Lat Pulldown Lab is Chapter 4.6, later). Content requirement for design: a labelled diagram of 6–8 major muscles, front and back views; a simple three-role diagram (prime mover / synergist / fixator) using a generic movement example, not a specific exercise.

**Self-check (ungraded, formative):**
- Labelling: given a diagram, label 6–8 major muscles.
- Matching: given a muscle name (e.g. gluteus medius, triceps brachii, frontalis), match it to the naming convention it illustrates (size, number of origins, location).
- Matching: given a described role in a movement, match it to prime mover, synergist, or fixator/stabiliser.

**Practical observation (safe self-application):** "Do one simple, pain-free movement — for example, bend your elbow slowly. Try to feel which muscle on the front of your upper arm is doing most of the work (the prime mover for this movement). This is noticing muscle activity in general terms, not assessing your strength, development or muscle health." No claim about how much the muscle is "working" in numeric or comparative terms is made or implied.

**Recall cards (spaced-repetition prompts):**
- Name three muscle-naming conventions and give an example of each.
- What is a prime mover? What is a fixator?
- What does the Lat Pulldown Lab call a "fixator"?

**Sources:** OpenStax, *Anatomy and Physiology 2e* (CC BY-NC-SA — see the licensing note on Lesson 1.1.1 and the open decision in [DECISIONS.md](../../DECISIONS.md#open) #12; the same commercial-use consideration applies here):
- [§11.1, "Interactions of Skeletal Muscles, Their Fascicle Arrangement, and Their Lever Systems"](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/11-1-interactions-of-skeletal-muscles-their-fascicle-arrangement-and-their-lever-systems) — prime mover/agonist, synergist, fixator, antagonist.
- [§11.2, "Naming Skeletal Muscles"](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/11-2-naming-skeletal-muscles) — naming conventions and the verified examples above.

Both sections were fetched and verified directly before writing, continuing the practice adopted after Lesson 1.1.1's initial mis-citation.

**Source limitations:** OpenStax's precise term for a stabilising synergist is "fixator," not "stabiliser" — the word "stabiliser" does not appear in §11.1. Fit360's own Lat Pulldown Lab spec (already approved and merged) chose "stabiliser" as a more accessible label for the same role; this lesson is explicit that the two terms describe the same concept rather than presenting "stabiliser" as if it were OpenStax's own word. This lesson makes no population-specific claim, so no Indian-population evidence caveat applies.

## Explicitly out of scope for this lesson

- No claim about any individual's muscle development, strength, imbalance or injury — this lesson only names and classifies muscles and their general roles.
- No exhaustive muscle list — only major, commonly referenced muscles are named; a full muscular-system inventory is out of scope at this introductory level.
- No exercise technique or programming content — that is Term 2 and beyond.
