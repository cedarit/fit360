## Chapter framing

**Why it matters:** Lesson 1.1.1 gave learners a fixed reference position and two body-region categories. This lesson gives them the rest of the vocabulary needed to describe *where* something is and *which way* a movement goes — vocabulary every later chapter (joint actions in Module 4, the Lat Pulldown Lab) depends on.

**Learning objectives:**
1. Use the directional terms anterior/posterior, superior/inferior, medial/lateral and proximal/distal correctly to describe a position.
2. Name the three cardinal planes (sagittal, frontal/coronal, transverse) and give an everyday movement example of motion in or parallel to each.
3. Distinguish a plane of motion from a joint action, at a preview level — full teaching of joint actions is Chapter 4.1.

**Learner capabilities (what a learner can do afterwards):**
- Given two points on a body diagram, correctly describe their relationship using a directional term pair.
- Watch a short movement clip and name which plane the movement mostly happens in.

**Prerequisites:** [1.1.1 — Anatomical position, body regions and organ systems](term-01-module-1-chapter-1-lesson-1.md).

**Keywords:** sagittal plane, frontal (coronal) plane, transverse plane, anterior, posterior, medial, lateral, superior, inferior, proximal, distal.

**Concept map (textual; a visual diagram is a later design task, not built here):**

**Safety and scope boundary:** this chapter describes position and plane vocabulary only; it never labels a movement as normal, abnormal, safe or unsafe for a specific person — that rule is set in Chapter 0.3 and applied fully once evaluative language becomes relevant in Chapter 4.5. Naming the plane of a movement is description, not assessment.

**Diagnostic quiz (ungraded, before teaching, to surface assumptions):**
1. True or False: "medial" and "lateral" describe up/down position. *(False — they describe toward/away from the midline.)*
2. Multiple choice: which term pair applies only to the limbs, not the whole body? (a) superior/inferior, (b) anterior/posterior, (c) proximal/distal. *(c.)*
3. True or False: a movement can only happen in exactly one plane and never involve more than one at once. *(False — sets up that many real movements combine planes, developed further in Module 4.)*

## Lesson body

**Question:** I know the anatomical position and the two body-region categories. How do I now describe exactly *where* something is, and *which way* a movement goes?

**Explanation:** Teach each directional term pair with its precise meaning (OpenStax, *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*, §1.6, "Anatomical Terminology"):
- **Anterior** — toward the front of the body; **posterior** — toward the back.
- **Superior** — above or higher than another part; **inferior** — below or lower.
- **Medial** — toward the middle of the body; **lateral** — toward the side.
- **Proximal** — nearer to the trunk along a limb; **distal** — farther from the trunk along a limb (this pair applies only within a limb, not to the whole body).

Then teach the three cardinal planes, again from OpenStax §1.6:
- **Sagittal plane** — divides the body vertically into right and left sides.
- **Frontal (coronal) plane** — divides the body into an anterior (front) and posterior (back) portion.
- **Transverse plane** — divides the body horizontally into upper and lower portions.

Give one everyday movement example per plane (these are Fit360's own illustrative teaching examples, not sourced facts, and are labelled as such):
- *Sagittal:* walking forward, or bending an elbow — the movement stays within, or parallel to, a plane dividing left from right.
- *Frontal/coronal:* raising both arms out to the sides, or stepping sideways — the movement is parallel to a plane dividing front from back.
- *Transverse:* turning your head to look over your shoulder, or twisting your torso — the movement is parallel to a plane dividing upper from lower.

Preview only, not full teaching: each of these example movements also has a name as a joint action (for example, "bending an elbow" is elbow flexion) — Chapter 4.1 teaches that vocabulary and connects it back to these planes in full.

**Visual/lab:** no interactive lab is attached to Chapter 1.2. Content requirement for design: a body diagram with three semi-transparent plane overlays (sagittal, frontal, transverse), toggleable one at a time, plus short looping video clips for the three example movements above.

**Self-check (ungraded, formative):**
- Labelling: given two labelled points on a body diagram, choose the correct directional term pair to describe their relationship.
- "Name the plane": given a short movement clip (or a text description, for the text-only version of this lesson), name which plane the movement mostly happens in.

**Practical observation (safe self-application):** "Perform (or picture) one simple, pain-free movement — for example raising one arm out to the side. Say out loud which plane it mostly happens in, using today's vocabulary." This is naming a plane, not assessing whether the movement is correct, safe or healthy — no form or injury-risk judgement is made.

**Recall cards (spaced-repetition prompts):**
- Give the four directional term pairs and what each one means.
- Which directional term pair applies only within a limb, and not to the body as a whole?
- Name the three cardinal planes and what each one divides.

**Sources:** OpenStax, *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*, [§1.6, "Anatomical Terminology"](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/1-6-anatomical-terminology) (CC BY-NC-SA — see the licensing note and open decision recorded on Lesson 1.1.1 and in [DECISIONS.md](../../DECISIONS.md#open) #11; the same commercial-use consideration applies here). Verified directly against this section, before citing it, that it states the exact definitions used above for all four directional term pairs and all three planes.

**Source limitations:** the three everyday movement examples per plane are Fit360's own illustrative teaching content, not claims from OpenStax or any other source, and are labelled as such above. They are deliberately simple, single-plane-dominant examples; Module 4 will note that many real movements combine more than one plane, which this lesson only flags as a preview (diagnostic quiz item 3), not a fully developed idea. This lesson makes no population-specific claim, so no Indian-population evidence caveat applies.

## Explicitly out of scope for this lesson

- No joint-action names (flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation) — that vocabulary, and its full connection to these planes, is Chapter 4.1.
- No specific bone, joint or muscle names.
- No discussion of any individual's body shape, posture, movement quality or health — this lesson names planes and directions, it never evaluates a movement.
