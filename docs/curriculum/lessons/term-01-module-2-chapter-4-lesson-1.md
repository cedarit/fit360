# Lesson 2.4.1 — Endocrine foundations, homeostasis and acute responses to activity

**Term 1, Module 2, Chapter 2.4, Lesson 1.** Completes Module 2. Term 1's most safety-sensitive physiology lesson — hormones and homeostasis sit closest to territory the safety policy explicitly excludes, so this lesson states that boundary more than once and applies it throughout.

## Chapter framing

**Why it matters:** learners will encounter the words "cortisol" and "adrenaline" constantly in fitness and wellness content, often attached to claims about an individual's stress or health. This lesson teaches what these hormones generally do, in the context of homeostasis — and is explicit, from the start, about what it will never do with that information.

**Learning objectives:**
1. Explain homeostasis using a movement-relevant example.
2. Name one or two hormones commonly discussed in exercise contexts (adrenaline/epinephrine, cortisol) and their general, qualitative role in an acute response — with no dosing, therapeutic or diagnostic claims.
3. Explain why Fit360 will never interpret an individual's hormone levels or test results.

**Learner capabilities (what a learner can do afterwards):**
- Explain homeostasis in one or two sentences, using a movement-relevant example.
- Name what adrenaline/epinephrine and cortisol are generally described as doing during an acute stress or activity response.
- State clearly why a lesson, a quiz, or an AI assistant response can never tell them what their own hormone levels or test results mean.

## Lesson body

**Question:** What is homeostasis, what do stress hormones like adrenaline and cortisol generally do, and — just as importantly — what will Fit360 never tell you about your own hormones?

**Explanation:**
- **Homeostasis** (OpenStax, *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*, §1.5, "Homeostasis"): the body continuously monitors its internal conditions and works to keep them within a stable, narrow range. This is regulated by a feedback loop with three parts: a **sensor** that monitors a value, a **control centre** that compares that value to the normal range, and an **effector** that acts to bring the value back if it's drifted too far. The standard example is body temperature, kept near a set point of about 37°C (98.6°F).
- **A movement-relevant example:** muscle contraction releases heat as it uses ATP — the same principle the source describes for shivering applies more generally to any muscle contraction, including voluntary movement. During activity, this added heat is exactly the kind of change the temperature feedback loop responds to, working to keep body temperature within its normal range even while a learner is active.
- **Acute hormone responses, qualitatively** (OpenStax, §17.6, "The Adrenal Glands"): adrenaline (epinephrine) is released as part of the fight-or-flight response, and its function is described as preparing the body for extreme physical exertion — a short-term response. Cortisol is involved in a longer-term stress response, generally described as helping mobilise the body's stored fuel (for example, breaking down stored glycogen and fat into forms the body can use for energy) to sustain a prolonged demand. No dose, level, or timeframe is given for either hormone, because none is needed at this introductory level and none would be honest to invent.
- **The boundary, restated:** knowing that cortisol "helps mobilise stored fuel" in general is medical literacy. Knowing what a specific cortisol test result means for one person is medical practice — and Fit360 does not do that, ever, regardless of who's asking or how the question is phrased.

**Visual/lab:** no interactive lab is attached to Chapter 2.4. Content requirement for design: a simple diagram of the three-part feedback loop (sensor → control centre → effector) using the temperature example, and a separate short-term/long-term timeline showing adrenaline's and cortisol's general roles side by side — no numeric axis, no hormone-level graph.
