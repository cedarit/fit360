# Lesson 3.2.1 — The three energy pathways: phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative

**Term 1, Module 3, Chapter 3.2, Lesson 1.** Builds directly on Lesson 3.1.1: now that a learner knows ATP must be continually resynthesised, this lesson explains the three general ways the body does that.

## Chapter framing

**Why it matters:** different activities — a maximal one-second effort, a hard minute, an easy hour — feel different because the body leans on different ways of resynthesising ATP. This lesson names those three general pathways and, unlike the last lesson, is allowed to use cited numeric ranges — as long as they're sourced and clearly flagged as ranges, not fixed universal cut-offs.

**Learning objectives:**
1. Name the three energy pathways and describe, qualitatively, the type of effort each suits.
2. State that specific duration cut-offs vary between sources and individuals, so Fit360 presents cited ranges rather than treating any single number as fixed.

**Learner capabilities (what a learner can do afterwards):**
- Name the three energy pathways.
- Match a described activity (a maximal short effort, a hard minute-long effort, a longer easy effort) to the pathway most likely dominating it.
- Explain why any duration range given for a pathway is a cited range, not a fixed personal rule.

**Prerequisites:** [3.1.1](term-01-module-3-chapter-1-lesson-1.md).

**Keywords:** phosphagen (ATP-PC) system, glycolytic system, oxidative system, anaerobic, aerobic.

**Concept map (textual; a visual diagram is a later design task, not built here):**

**Safety and scope boundary:** this lesson never tells a learner which pathway is dominant *for them* during any activity they do — it teaches the general concept and cited ranges only. No self-application activity exists for this chapter.

2. Multiple choice: which pathway is best suited to a maximal effort lasting only a few seconds? (a) oxidative, (b) glycolytic, (c) phosphagen. *(c.)*
3. True or False: the oxidative system requires a steady oxygen supply to keep working. *(True.)*

## Lesson body

**Question:** My body needs to keep remaking ATP no matter what I'm doing — so how does it do that differently for a one-second effort versus a slow one-hour walk?

**Explanation** (OpenStax, *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*, §10.3, "Muscle Fiber Contraction and Relaxation" — note: OpenStax describes these processes by their metabolic names, not as "phosphagen/glycolytic/oxidative systems"; those are Fit360's own chapter vocabulary, mapped below to the source's actual terms):
- **Phosphagen system** (OpenStax: creatine phosphate) — powers the very first moments of effort. Per this source, creatine phosphate "can only provide approximately 15 seconds worth of energy," covering "the first few seconds of muscle contraction." Best suited to very short, high-intensity efforts.
- **Glycolytic system** (OpenStax: anaerobic glycolysis) — takes over as the phosphagen system's short window closes. Per this source, "glycolysis itself cannot be sustained for very long (approximately 1 minute of muscle activity)," and is "useful in facilitating short bursts of high-intensity output." Best suited to short-to-moderate, high-intensity efforts.
- **Oxidative system** (OpenStax: aerobic respiration) — the main long-run supplier. Per this source, "approximately 95 percent of the ATP required for resting or moderately active muscles is provided by aerobic respiration," but it "cannot be sustained without a steady supply of O2." Best suited to longer, lower-intensity efforts, where oxygen delivery (Lesson 2.3.1) can keep up with demand.

**These figures are a cited range from one textbook source, not a fixed personal rule.** Other authoritative sources (for example, professional-organisation position stands) may state somewhat different cut-offs, and any individual's actual energy-system contribution during a specific activity isn't something this lesson — or any simple test — can measure. The pattern (short/fast → phosphagen; short-to-moderate/fast → glycolytic; longer/slower → oxidative) is the durable, general takeaway; the exact second-counts are illustrative, cited ranges.
