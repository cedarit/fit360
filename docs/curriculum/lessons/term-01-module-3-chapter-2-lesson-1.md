# Lesson 3.2.1 — The three energy pathways: phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative

**Term 1, Module 3, Chapter 3.2, Lesson 1.** Builds directly on Lesson 3.1.1: now that a learner knows ATP must be continually resynthesised, this lesson explains the three general ways the body does that.

## Chapter framing

**Why it matters:** different activities — a maximal one-second effort, a hard minute, an easy hour — feel different because the body leans on different ways of resynthesising ATP. This lesson names those three general pathways and, unlike the last lesson, is allowed to use cited numeric figures — as long as they're sourced and clearly flagged as one source's approximate estimates, not fixed universal cut-offs or intervals.

**Learning objectives:**
1. Name the three energy pathways and describe, qualitatively, the type of effort each suits.
2. State that specific duration figures vary between sources and individuals, so Fit360 presents them as one source's approximate estimates rather than treating any single number as fixed.

**Learner capabilities (what a learner can do afterwards):**
- Name the three energy pathways.
- Match a described activity (a maximal short effort, a hard minute-long effort, a longer easy effort) to the pathway most likely dominating it.
- Explain why any duration figure given for a pathway is one source's approximate estimate, not a fixed personal rule.

**Prerequisites:** [3.1.1](term-01-module-3-chapter-1-lesson-1.md).

**Keywords:** phosphagen (ATP-PC) system, glycolytic system, oxidative system, anaerobic, aerobic.

## Lesson body

**Question:** My body needs to keep remaking ATP no matter what I'm doing — so how does it do that differently for a one-second effort versus a slow one-hour walk?

**Explanation** (OpenStax, *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*, §10.3, "Muscle Fiber Contraction and Relaxation" — note: OpenStax describes these processes by their metabolic names, not as "phosphagen/glycolytic/oxidative systems"; those are Fit360's own chapter vocabulary, mapped below to the source's actual terms):
- **Phosphagen system** (OpenStax: creatine phosphate) — powers the very first moments of effort. Per this source, creatine phosphate "can only provide approximately 15 seconds worth of energy," covering "the first few seconds of muscle contraction." Best suited to very short, high-intensity efforts.
- **Glycolytic system** (OpenStax: anaerobic glycolysis) — typically becomes the more prominent contributor as effort continues beyond the phosphagen system's brief window, without this lesson claiming a strict on/off switch between the two. Per this source, "glycolysis itself cannot be sustained for very long (approximately 1 minute of muscle activity)," and is "useful in facilitating short bursts of high-intensity output." Best suited to short-to-moderate, high-intensity efforts.
- **Oxidative system** (OpenStax: aerobic respiration) — the main long-run supplier. Per this source, "approximately 95 percent of the ATP required for resting or moderately active muscles is provided by aerobic respiration," but it "cannot be sustained without a steady supply of O2." Best suited to longer, lower-intensity efforts, where oxygen delivery (Lesson 2.3.1) can keep up with demand. The 95% figure applies specifically to resting or moderately active muscle, per this source — not to maximal or high-intensity effort.

**These figures are approximate estimates from one textbook source, not a fixed personal rule, and not stated by that source as ranges or intervals.** Other authoritative sources (for example, professional-organisation position stands) may state somewhat different figures, and any individual's actual energy-system contribution during a specific activity isn't something this lesson — or any simple test — can measure. The pattern (short/fast → phosphagen more prominent; short-to-moderate/fast → glycolytic more prominent; longer/slower → oxidative more prominent) is the durable, general takeaway, describing typical relative dominance rather than an exclusive switch; the exact second-counts and percentage are illustrative, single-source estimates.

**Visual/lab:** no interactive lab is attached to Chapter 3.2. Content requirement for design: a simple timeline showing each pathway's typical relative dominance shifting gradually across effort duration — not discrete, non-overlapping blocks — explicitly labelled as one source's approximate estimates rather than a precise personal chart or a claim that the pathways switch on and off exclusively.
