# Lesson 0.2.1 — How to judge a claim

**Term 1, Module 0, Chapter 0.2, Lesson 1.** A complete, reviewable lesson outline for the second Module 0 lesson, following Lesson [0.1.1](term-01-module-0-chapter-1-lesson-1.md)'s pattern.

## Chapter framing

**Why it matters:** learners will constantly meet competing claims about health, fitness and nutrition — from social media, advertisements, well-meaning friends and sometimes conflicting official advice. Fit360 cannot teach every fact a learner will ever encounter, but it can teach the skill of judging a claim's strength. That skill is used in every later term, and is revisited explicitly before Term 5 (Evidence).

**Learning objectives:**
1. Rank source types by strength using Fit360's evidence hierarchy: primary sources and authoritative guidelines, then systematic reviews and meta-analyses, then established textbooks, then professional organisations, then Fit360's own explanations and labs.
2. Explain why evidence gathered from one population (for example, mostly non-Indian participants) doesn't automatically transfer to another population.
3. Apply the hierarchy and the population-validity check to a simple example claim, and state a confidence level with reasoning.

**Learner capabilities (what a learner can do afterwards):**
- Look at two sources making the same claim and say which one deserves more trust, and why.
- Say "this evidence is mostly from elsewhere, so I'm not certain it applies exactly to Indian bodies or diets" when that's the honest position.

**Prerequisites:** [0.1 — What Fit360 is and isn't](term-01-module-0-chapter-1-lesson-1.md).

**Keywords:** evidence hierarchy, systematic review, meta-analysis, primary source, conflict of interest, population validity, certainty language.

**Concept map (textual; a visual diagram is a later design task, not built here):**
```
Evidence hierarchy (strongest → weakest)
  1. Primary sources & authoritative guidelines (incl. Indian bodies such as ICMR, where relevant)
  2. Systematic reviews & meta-analyses
  3. Established textbooks
  4. Professional organisations
  5. Fit360's own explanations & labs (always traced back to 1–4)
        ↓
  Population-validity check: who was this evidence actually about?
        ↓
  Certainty language: "strong evidence" / "limited evidence" / "mixed evidence" / "we don't know yet"
```

**Safety and scope boundary:** this chapter teaches learners to judge *general* claims they encounter in the world. It never asks a learner to evaluate their own test results, symptoms or medical situation — that boundary belongs to Chapter 0.1 (what Fit360 will never do) and Chapter 0.3 (the standing referral rule and emergency pathway). If a learner brings their own results into this lesson's activities, redirect to those chapters rather than judging the result's meaning here.

**Diagnostic quiz (ungraded, before teaching, to surface assumptions):**
1. True or False: one person's personal experience or testimonial is generally stronger evidence than a systematic review of many studies. *(False.)*
2. Multiple choice: which generally sits higher in Fit360's evidence hierarchy — (a) a professional organisation's position statement, or (b) a systematic review of multiple trials? *(b), as a general rule — the lesson also covers why real-world judgement calls exist.)*
3. True or False: if a study is conducted only on one population, its findings automatically apply exactly to every other population too. *(False — sets up the population-validity check.)*

## Lesson body

**Question:** Two sources disagree about a health or fitness claim. How do I decide which one — if either — to trust?

**Explanation:** Walk through Fit360's five-tier evidence hierarchy (from [PROJECT_CONTEXT.md](../../PROJECT_CONTEXT.md#4-evidence-policy)). The ranking itself — systematic reviews and meta-analyses outranking a single study or textbook — is not a Fit360 invention; it mirrors established evidence-based-medicine methodology, for example the [Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine's Levels of Evidence](https://www.cebm.ox.ac.uk/resources/levels-of-evidence/oxford-centre-for-evidence-based-medicine-levels-of-evidence-march-2009) and the GRADE approach to grading evidence quality. PROJECT_CONTEXT.md adapts that general methodology into Fit360's own five practical tiers (adding, for instance, "Fit360's own explanations and labs" as tier 5, which CEBM/GRADE don't cover, since they grade research evidence, not an education product's own content). One line of reasoning per tier:
1. Primary sources and authoritative guidelines (including Indian bodies such as ICMR where relevant) — closest to the actual research or official recommendation.
2. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses — combine many primary studies, which reduces the effect of any single study's flaws, bias or small sample size.
3. Established textbooks — synthesised and vetted, but can lag behind the newest research.
4. Professional organisations — practical and current, but may reflect institutional interests alongside evidence.
5. Fit360's own explanations and interactive labs — always built from tiers 1–4 and linked to their sources; never the authority itself.

Then teach the **population-validity check**: ask "who was this evidence actually about?" Evidence gathered mostly from non-Indian populations doesn't automatically transfer — differences in diet, body composition, genetics and environment can matter. Fit360 says so explicitly whenever this applies, rather than presenting foreign-population findings as universal.

Then teach **certainty language**: "strong evidence," "limited evidence," "mixed evidence," "we don't know yet." Model honest hedging as a strength, not a weakness — a claim stated with appropriate uncertainty is more trustworthy than one stated with false confidence.

**Visual/lab:** no interactive lab is attached to Module 0. Content requirement for design: a five-rung "ladder" graphic for the evidence hierarchy, with a magnifying-glass icon beside it for the population-validity check. No real study logos, journal names or brand marks, to avoid implying endorsement of (or by) any specific real source.

**Self-check (ungraded, formative):** present three claims, each explicitly labelled as a fictional example and given a source type (not a real citation), and ask the learner to (a) assign a confidence level — strong / limited / mixed / unclear — and (b) note whether population validity is a concern.
- *Example A (fictional):* "A wellness blog post claims a specific breathing pattern changes how the body uses energy." Source: a single blog post, no citation. → Low confidence; no systematic evidence, no population data.
- *Example B (fictional):* "A systematic review of multiple trials reports that regular resistance training is associated with improved general strength outcomes in adults." Source: systematic review. → Higher confidence given the source type; still worth checking which populations the trials studied.
- *Example C (fictional):* "A supplement company's own website states its product improves recovery, citing the company's internal report." Source: company-authored internal report. → Low confidence; conflict of interest, no independent review.

These examples are templates for practising the skill — they do not name a real study, brand, product or numeric result, and must never be reused elsewhere as if they were real evidence.

**Practical observation (non-clinical, everyday-life task):** "Next time you see a health or fitness claim — an advertisement, a video, a forum post — note: what kind of source is it? Does it say who was studied? Write one line stating your confidence level (strong / limited / mixed / unclear) and why." This asks learners to evaluate claims in the world around them, never their own body or medical situation.

**Recall cards (spaced-repetition prompts):**
- List Fit360's five evidence-hierarchy tiers in order, strongest to weakest.
- Why doesn't evidence from one population always transfer directly to another?
- Name Fit360's four certainty-language terms.

**Sources:** Fit360's specific five-tier structure and its "Fit360's own explanations" tier are Fit360's own policy, approved by Pankaj in [PROJECT_CONTEXT.md](../../PROJECT_CONTEXT.md#4-evidence-policy) — not an external claim, so that document is the primary source for the tiers as Fit360 defines them, same as Lesson 0.1.1. The underlying *methodology* — that systematic reviews/meta-analyses rank above a single study, textbook or organisational statement — is an established evidence-based-medicine convention, not something Fit360 asserts on its own authority; see the [Oxford CEBM Levels of Evidence (March 2009)](https://www.cebm.ox.ac.uk/resources/levels-of-evidence/oxford-centre-for-evidence-based-medicine-levels-of-evidence-march-2009) and the [GRADE Working Group](https://www.gradeworkinggroup.org/) approach, cited here as methodological grounding rather than reproduced. The three self-check examples are explicitly fictional templates, not real citations, and carry no real study, product or numeric claim.

## Explicitly out of scope for this lesson

- No real named study, product, organisation or brand in any example — all three self-check claims are generic and fictional.
- No numeric result, percentage or statistic, even inside a fictional example.
- No evaluation of a learner's own test results, symptoms or medical situation — redirect to Chapters 0.1 and 0.3 if this comes up.
- No anatomy, physiology or biomechanics content — that starts in Module 1.
