# Lesson 2.2.1 — Muscle structure, contraction and fibre types

**Term 1, Module 2, Chapter 2.2, Lesson 1.** Builds on Chapter 1.4's muscle naming and Chapter 2.1's nervous-system introduction.

## Chapter framing

**Why it matters:** muscle-fibre-type claims — "you're mostly fast-twitch," often with an invented percentage — are extremely common in popular fitness content, and almost always unfounded for an individual without direct laboratory testing. This lesson teaches the real, general concept accurately, precisely so a learner can recognise when a claim like that is overstated.

**Learning objectives:**
1. Describe the general structure of skeletal muscle (muscle → fascicle → fibre) and the sliding-filament concept, qualitatively.
2. Distinguish, at an introductory level, the general characteristics of slower, more fatigue-resistant fibres versus faster, more powerful fibres — without asserting any individual's fibre-type proportions.
3. Explain why fibre type is a population-level generalisation that Fit360 cannot measure or claim for an individual learner.

**Learner capabilities (what a learner can do afterwards):**
- Describe, in general terms, how a muscle is built from smaller and smaller units down to the fibre.
- Explain the sliding-filament idea in one or two sentences.
- Explain why nobody can honestly tell a learner their personal fibre-type percentages from a general lesson, a quiz, or a simple test.

**Prerequisites:** [1.4.1](term-01-module-1-chapter-4-lesson-1.md), [2.1.1](term-01-module-2-chapter-1-lesson-1.md).

**Keywords:** skeletal muscle, fascicle, sliding filament concept, fibre type (Type I / Type II, qualitative only).

**Concept map (textual; a visual diagram is a later design task, not built here):**
```
Whole muscle (wrapped in connective tissue)
   └─ Fascicle — a bundle of muscle fibres
         └─ Muscle fibre — an individual muscle cell
                └─ Sarcomere — the unit that shortens
                       (thick filaments/myosin slide past
                        thin filaments/actin — the "sliding
                        filament" concept)

Fibre types (qualitative, population-level only)
   ├─ Slower, more fatigue-resistant fibres — suited to sustained, lower-force activity
   └─ Faster, more powerful fibres — suited to brief, higher-force activity
          (a real intermediate type exists too — see "source limitations")
   Every muscle contains a mix, in proportions that vary by muscle and
   by person — not something a lesson, quiz or simple test can measure.
```

**Safety and scope boundary:** this chapter never tells a learner what their own fibre-type mix is, and never implies a simple test (a jump, a rep-max guess, or similar) can reveal it. Fibre type here is taught as population-level physiology, not personal information.

**Diagnostic quiz (ungraded, before teaching, to surface assumptions):**
1. True or False: a simple test (like counting how high you can jump) can reliably tell you your personal fibre-type percentages. *(False — sets up the whole lesson.)*
2. Multiple choice: the structure that bundles individual muscle fibres together is the — (a) sarcomere, (b) fascicle, (c) tendon. *(b.)*
3. True or False: every muscle contains only one fibre type. *(False — muscles contain a mix.)*

## Lesson body

**Question:** What is a muscle actually made of, how does it shorten, and is it really true that some people are "born fast-twitch"?

**Explanation:**
- **Structure, from whole muscle to fibre** (OpenStax, *Anatomy and Physiology 2e*, §10.2, "Skeletal Muscle"): a whole muscle is organised into bundles called **fascicles**, each wrapped in connective tissue; each fascicle is made of individual **muscle fibres** (muscle cells).
- **The sliding-filament concept** (OpenStax, §10.3, "Muscle Fiber Contraction and Relaxation"): inside each fibre, a repeating unit called the sarcomere contains thick filaments (made of the protein myosin) and thin filaments (made of the protein actin). When signalled by a motor neuron, the thin filaments are pulled and slide past the thick filaments, shortening the sarcomere — this is the "sliding filament" concept.
- **Fibre types, qualitatively** (OpenStax, §10.5, "Types of Muscle Fibers"): fibres differ in how fast they contract and how they produce energy. Slower, more fatigue-resistant fibres (OpenStax calls these "slow oxidative") are well suited to sustained, lower-force activity like maintaining posture. Faster, more powerful fibres (OpenStax calls these "fast glycolytic") produce quick, forceful contractions but tire quickly, suited to brief, high-effort activity. **Every muscle contains a mix of fibre types, and OpenStax is explicit that the exact proportions vary by muscle and are not given as a fixed number for any "typical" person** — there is no percentage to quote, for anyone.

**Visual/lab:** no interactive lab is attached to Chapter 2.2. Content requirement for design: a simple animated zoom (whole muscle → fascicle → fibre → sarcomere) and a short looping animation of filaments sliding past each other.

**Self-check (ungraded, formative):**
- True/false-with-explanation: for example, "A single muscle is made of only one type of fibre" (false, with the reason).
- Match-the-description: given a description of contraction speed and fatigue resistance, match it to "slower, fatigue-resistant" or "faster, powerful" — no numeric or percentage-based questions, per this chapter's own assessment rule.

**Practical observation (safe self-application):** "Think of two different activities — for example, standing still for a long time, versus a short, all-out effort like a fast sprint for a few seconds. Describe, in general terms, which fibre characteristics (slower/fatigue-resistant, or faster/powerful) are probably doing more of the work in each activity, and why." This is applying the *general concept* to example activities — it is explicitly **not** a self-test of the learner's own fibre-type mix, and this lesson does not offer or imply any such test exists.

**Recall cards (spaced-repetition prompts):**
- Describe the structure of a muscle from whole muscle down to the sarcomere.
- What is the sliding-filament concept, in one sentence?
- Why can't Fit360 (or anyone, without lab testing) tell you your personal fibre-type percentages?

**Sources:** OpenStax, *Anatomy and Physiology 2e* (CC BY-NC-SA — see the licensing note on Lesson 1.1.1 and the open decision in [DECISIONS.md](../../DECISIONS.md#open) #12; the same commercial-use consideration applies here):
- [§10.2, "Skeletal Muscle"](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-2-skeletal-muscle) — the muscle → fascicle → fibre hierarchy.
- [§10.3, "Muscle Fiber Contraction and Relaxation"](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-3-muscle-fiber-contraction-and-relaxation) — the sliding filament model.
- [§10.5, "Types of Muscle Fibers"](https://openstax.org/books/anatomy-and-physiology-2e/pages/10-5-types-of-muscle-fibers) — fibre type characteristics, and the explicit absence of any percentage breakdown.

All three sections were fetched and verified directly before writing.

**Source limitations:** OpenStax actually describes **three** fibre types — slow oxidative (Type I), fast oxidative (Type IIa), and fast glycolytic (Type IIx) — not the simpler two-category "slower vs faster" split this lesson teaches. The two-category framing follows this chapter's own introductory-level learning outcome; the intermediate "fast oxidative" type is real and is flagged here rather than silently omitted. §10.5 explicitly states muscles contain a mix of all three types "in varying proportions," with no percentage given for any individual or typical person — that sentence is the direct textual basis for this lesson's central safety point, not an inference. This lesson makes no population-specific (Indian vs. non-Indian) claim, so no such evidence caveat applies here.

## Explicitly out of scope for this lesson

- No percentage, proportion, ratio or any other number describing an individual's or a "typical" person's fibre-type mix.
- No self-test, quiz result, or simple physical test framed as revealing a learner's personal fibre-type mix — no such test is offered or implied to exist.
- No claims about an individual's athletic potential, genetics, or training response based on fibre type.
- No detailed molecular biology of contraction beyond the qualitative sliding-filament concept.
