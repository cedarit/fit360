# Lesson 4.3.1 — Balance, centre of gravity and stability

**Term 1, Module 4, Chapter 4.3, Lesson 1.** Builds on the force and torque vocabulary from Lesson [4.2.1](term-01-module-4-chapter-2-lesson-1.md) to explain, in general terms, what makes a stance or position more or less stable.

## Chapter framing

**Why it matters:** Lessons 4.1.1 and 4.2.1 gave learners vocabulary for individual joint movements and the forces behind them. This lesson zooms out to the whole body: why some stances feel steadier than others, and what two general factors explain most of that difference. This is purely descriptive vocabulary — it never assesses whether a specific learner's own balance is good, bad or at risk.

**Learning objectives:**
1. Define centre of gravity and base of support in plain language.
2. Explain their general relationship to balance.
3. Describe qualitatively what affects stability in a simple stance or movement.

**Learner capabilities (what a learner can do afterwards):**
- Explain in one sentence what centre of gravity is and what base of support is.
- Given a diagram or description of two stances, predict in general terms which is more stable and explain why, using today's vocabulary.

**Prerequisites:** [Lesson 4.1.1 — Joint actions and planes of motion, applied](term-01-module-4-chapter-1-lesson-1.md); [Lesson 4.2.1 — Force, torque and leverage](term-01-module-4-chapter-2-lesson-1.md).

**Keywords:** centre of gravity, base of support, stable equilibrium, unstable equilibrium, balance.

**Concept map (textual; a visual diagram is a later design task, not built here):**
```
Centre of gravity (cg) — the point through which a body's weight
effectively acts
   │
   ▼
Base of support — the area on the ground bounded by points of contact
(for example, the space between and around the feet in standing)
   │
   ▼
While the cg stays above the base of support → stable
(a small displacement produces a restoring effect back toward balance)
   │
If the cg moves outside the base of support → unstable
(the displacement grows instead of being corrected — a fall)
   │
   ▼
Two general factors that increase stability:
   ├─ A wider base of support (for example, feet spread farther apart)
   └─ A lower centre of gravity (for example, bending the knees)
```

**Safety and scope boundary:** this chapter teaches general balance and stability vocabulary; it never assesses an individual's own balance, fall risk or stability as good, bad, safe or unsafe. Per the Module 0 boundary (see Lesson [0.3.1](term-01-module-0-chapter-3-lesson-1.md)), any fall risk, dizziness or instability concern a learner raises is routed to the standing referral line, never treated as something to "test" or self-diagnose through a lesson activity.

**Diagnostic quiz (ungraded, before teaching, to surface assumptions):**
1. True or False: standing with your feet close together is generally more stable than standing with them spread apart. *(False — sets up that a wider base of support generally increases stability.)*
2. Multiple choice: a football player bends their knees before receiving a ball mainly because this — (a) makes them taller, (b) lowers their centre of gravity and increases stability, (c) has no effect on balance. *(b.)*
3. True or False: instability happens the moment the centre of gravity moves at all, even slightly. *(False — sets up that small displacements are normally self-correcting, and instability specifically follows once the centre of gravity moves outside the base of support.)*

## Lesson body

**Question:** Why does spreading your feet apart, or bending your knees, make you feel steadier?

**Explanation:** OpenStax, *College Physics 2e*, §9.3, "Stability":

- **Stable vs. unstable equilibrium:** "A system is said to be in stable equilibrium if, when displaced from equilibrium, it experiences a net force or torque in a direction opposite to the direction of the displacement," moving it back toward balance. By contrast, "a system is in unstable equilibrium if, when displaced, it experiences a net force or torque in the same direction as the displacement," so the displacement grows instead of correcting itself.
- **The critical point:** for a standing person, "the critical point is reached when the [centre of gravity] is no longer above the base of support." While it stays above the base of support, small displacements are generally self-correcting; once it moves outside that base, the person becomes unstable.
- **Two general factors that increase stability:** "For increased stability while standing, the feet should be spread apart, giving a larger base of support." Separately, "stability is also increased by lowering one's centre of gravity by bending the knees, as when a football player prepares to receive a ball or braces themselves for a tackle." *(Directly answers the opening question: a wider base of support and a lower centre of gravity are the two general factors.)*
- **Everyday application:** "A cane, a crutch, or a walker increases the stability of the user, even more as the base of support widens" — the same base-of-support principle applied to a mobility aid rather than the feet alone.

**Terminology bridge:** Fit360's own chapter architecture uses "centre of mass"; the verified source for this lesson consistently uses "center of gravity" and does not state that the two terms are interchangeable in this book. This lesson therefore teaches "centre of gravity," matching the cited source precisely, and notes the "centre of mass" wording here as the same general point-of-balance idea rather than asserting a physics equivalence this source doesn't establish.

**Visual/lab:** no interactive lab is attached to Chapter 4.3. Content requirement for design: a simple animated diagram showing a standing figure's centre of gravity and base of support, with the cg staying above the base during small sways (stable) versus moving outside it during a larger lean (unstable) — paired with a side-by-side "feet together vs. feet apart" and "standing tall vs. knees bent" comparison.

**Self-check (ungraded, formative):**
- Short-answer/diagram exercise: given two simple stances (for example, feet together vs. feet apart), predict which is more stable and explain why, in general terms.
- Concept check: "What are the two general factors that increase stability in a stance?"

**Practical observation (safe self-application):** "Stand in a simple, low-risk stance (for example, feet together) and then widen your stance. Notice, in general terms, whether one feels steadier — without judging your own balance as good or bad." Any dizziness, fall risk or instability concern noticed during this activity is not a "test" result to interpret; it is routed to the standing referral line, per Chapter 0.3.

**Recall cards (spaced-repetition prompts):**
- What is a base of support?
- What are the two general factors that increase stability in a stance?
- What happens once the centre of gravity moves outside the base of support?

## Explicitly out of scope for this lesson
